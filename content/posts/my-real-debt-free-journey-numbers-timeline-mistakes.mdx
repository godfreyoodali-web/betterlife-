Most debt free journey posts show you the after photo. The confetti, the final payment screenshot, the neat little graph trending down to zero. Mine has all of that too, but I want to show you the messy middle first, because that's the part that actually got skipped in every post I read while I was drowning in my own numbers.

I started at $34,600 across credit cards, a personal loan, and a car loan I regretted the day I signed for it. I finished at zero, but it took three years, one relapse into new debt, and a lot of months that looked nothing like the tidy progress charts I kept comparing myself to.

## Where the $34,600 Actually Came From

$14,200 sat on three credit cards, accumulated over about two years of small "I'll pay it off next month" decisions. A $9,400 personal loan covered a period of underemployment I don't talk about much, since it still feels a little raw. The remaining $11,000 was a car loan on a vehicle that was, in hindsight, more car than I needed at the time.

None of it came from one bad decision. It came from a hundred small ones that felt reasonable individually and became unmanageable together.

## Year One: The Motivated, Messy Start

I attacked this with everything I had for the first four months, cutting my spending aggressively and throwing every spare dollar at the smallest balance first. I paid off $6,800 in those four months, which felt incredible and completely unsustainable at the same time.

By month five, I burned out. I'd restricted so hard that I overspent for two straight weeks trying to compensate, adding $1,400 back onto a card I'd just paid down. That relapse is the part I skipped telling people about for a long time, because it felt like proof I couldn't actually do this.

## Year Two: Slower, but It Actually Stuck

I rebuilt the plan around something sustainable instead of something dramatic. I picked a percentage of my income, not a fixed dollar amount, to send toward debt each month, which flexed naturally with months that were tighter than others. This is the version of the plan that actually survived.

I also picked up freelance work on weekends, bringing in an extra $300 to $500 a month depending on how much I took on, and every dollar of it went straight to the debt with no exceptions. By the end of year two, the balance was down to $9,200, almost entirely from the credit cards and personal loan being cleared.

## Year Three: The Car Loan and the Finish

The car loan was the last piece, and honestly the one I resented paying off the most, since it represented a decision I wished I'd made differently in the first place. I sold some things I didn't need, cut a few subscriptions I'd forgotten about, and put an unexpected small inheritance from a relative directly toward the balance instead of anywhere else.

I made the final payment fourteen months after starting year three, thirty seven months total from that first honest look at the number on my kitchen table.

## The Mistakes I'd Actually Warn You About

Going too aggressive too early set me up for the relapse in month five. A plan you can't sustain for more than four months isn't actually a plan, it's a sprint that burns out before the debt does.

I also didn't build any emergency fund alongside the debt payoff for the first year and a half, which meant a car repair in month nine went straight back onto a credit card I'd been working to pay down. A small cushion, even $500, would have prevented that entire step backward.

## What I'd Do Differently

I'd build a small emergency fund first, even a modest one, before going all in on debt payoff. I'd also start with a sustainable percentage based plan instead of an aggressive fixed amount, since the version that actually finished the job was the slower, steadier one, not the dramatic four month sprint.

## Where I Actually Landed

$34,600 to zero in thirty seven months, including one relapse, one car repair setback, and a pace that looked nothing like the debt free journey stories I was reading while I was still in the middle of my own. The math wasn't linear. The progress wasn't either.

If you're in the middle of your own number right now, the messy months don't mean you're failing. They're just the part nobody puts in the after photo.

My daughter asked me once why we don't just get more money from the bank when we need it, and I gave her the kind of vague, deflecting answer that felt easier in the moment than the actual explanation. I've since decided that deflecting was the wrong call, even for a question from a seven year old.

If you're searching how to talk to kids about money and getting advice that's either too abstract or too heavy for a kid to actually hold, here's what I've landed on after several years of getting it wrong before getting it slightly less wrong.

## Why I Stopped Deflecting

Kids notice money tension whether you name it or not. Mine picked up on stress in a grocery store checkout line long before she had the vocabulary to ask about it directly. Silence didn't protect her from that tension. It just left her to fill in the blanks herself, usually with something scarier than the truth.

That realization is what actually changed how I approach this, more than any parenting article did.

## What I Actually Say, by Age and Situation

### When She Asks Why We Can't Buy Something

I say the real reason, scaled to what she can hold. Not "we can't afford it" delivered like a closed door, but "that's not what this month's money is going toward, and here's what it is going toward instead." It turns a no into information instead of a wall.

### When She Notices Money Stress Between Me and My Partner

I don't pretend the tension isn't there, since she already knows it is. I say something like "we're figuring out a bill together, and that's a normal part of having money," which names the reality without handing her the full weight of adult financial stress.

### When She Asks How Much I Make

This one still catches me off guard every time. I've landed on giving her a real, simplified answer rather than deflecting, since deflecting taught her that money is a topic to whisper about rather than understand. The specific number matters less than the fact that the question gets a real answer.

### When She Wants Something Her Friend Has

I resist the urge to say "we just don't have the money," which quietly frames money as a source of lack rather than a tool. Instead I talk about priorities, that our family is choosing to put money toward different things right now, which is true and doesn't attach shame to either family's choices.

## The Mistakes I've Actually Made

I used to swing hard toward transparency without calibrating it to her age, once explaining our mortgage in more detail than a six year old needed or wanted. I've also gone too far the other direction, staying so vague that she filled the silence with an assumption that we were in worse shape than we actually were.

Neither extreme worked. What's working now is somewhere in the uncomfortable middle, real information without adult sized weight attached to it.

## What I'm Actually Trying to Teach Her

Not a specific budgeting method or a savings account balance. I want her to grow up understanding that money is discussable, that needing to make a tradeoff isn't a family failure, and that asking a direct question about money will get her a direct answer instead of a deflection.

That last part is the one I think about most, since deflection is exactly what I inherited growing up, and it took me well into adulthood to unlearn the anxiety that came with it.

## If You're Trying to Figure This Out Too

Start by answering the next money question your kid asks with something real, scaled to what they can actually hold, instead of a reflexive deflection. You don't need a perfect script. You need to be willing to treat the question as a real one instead of something to redirect.

Talking to kids about money honestly was never about getting every conversation right. It was about making money something we could actually talk about in this house, instead of something we quietly worked around.

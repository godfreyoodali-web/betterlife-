My credit card debt didn't happen because of one bad decision. It built up over two years of small "I'll pay it off next month" moments that quietly turned into $10,412 spread across three cards, all carrying interest between 19 and 24 percent.
If you're looking up how to pay off credit card debt and feeling that specific mix of dread and denial that comes with three separate minimum payments, I've been exactly there. Here's the actual plan that got me to zero, not a theoretical one.


**Facing the Real Number First**


I avoided logging into two of the three accounts for almost three months because I didn't want to see the total. When I finally added everything up, the relief of knowing the real number outweighed the fear of seeing it. Vague dread is worse than an exact figure you can actually plan against.
$10,412. Writing it down made it a project instead of a weight I carried around without a plan.


**The Order I Attacked the Cards In**


I used the avalanche method, ranking my cards by interest rate instead of balance. My highest rate card, sitting at 24 percent, got every extra dollar first while the other two received minimum payments only. Mathematically this saves the most money, though I'll admit the debt snowball method's quick wins would have felt better some months when progress felt invisible.


**I Paid the Highest Interest Card First, No Matter the Balance**


That 24 percent card wasn't my largest balance, but it was quietly costing me more per month than either of the other two. Attacking it first meant the interest stopped compounding against me as aggressively as it had been.


**I Called Each Card Issuer and Asked for a Lower Rate**


This felt awkward the first time, reading a script off my notes app to a stranger on the phone. Two of the three issuers actually lowered my rate when I asked directly, one from 24 percent down to 18, which meant more of every payment went toward the actual balance instead of interest.


**I Stopped Using the Cards Entirely, Not Just "Mostly"**


I'd told myself I'd only use them for emergencies, which slowly redefined itself to include things that weren't emergencies at all. I moved the physical cards into a drawer I rarely opened and removed them from every saved payment method online.


**Where the Extra Payments Actually Came From**


None of this happened on my regular paycheck alone. I picked up freelance proofreading on weekends, bringing in an extra $280 most months, and every dollar of it went straight to the highest interest card with no exceptions.
I also renegotiated my car insurance, which saved $45 a month for doing nothing more than a fifteen minute phone call. Small, unglamorous, and it added up faster than I expected.


**The Month I Almost Gave Up**


Around month seven, a dental emergency cost me $600 I hadn't planned for, and I had to put it on the card I'd just paid down. That felt like proof the whole plan was failing. It wasn't. One setback in a year long project doesn't erase the other eleven months of progress, even though it genuinely felt that way at the time.


**The Real Timeline**


I paid off the 24 percent card, later reduced to 18, in month five. The second card took another four months. The last card, which had the lowest interest rate of the three, took the remaining three months. Total time from that first honest look at my accounts to a zero balance across all three cards: twelve months.
$10,412 to zero in a year, including one unplanned $600 setback along the way that didn't derail the plan, just slowed it down for a few weeks.


**If You're Staring at Your Own Number Right Now**


Look at the real total before anything else. Rank your cards by interest rate, not balance, and call every issuer to ask for a lower rate, because it works more often than you'd expect. Find one extra income source, even a small one, and send every dollar of it to the card costing you the most.
Paying off credit card debt was never about being perfect for twelve straight months. It was about building a plan sturdy enough to survive the one month that didn't go as planned.
